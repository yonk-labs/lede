The European Central Bank announced on Tuesday that it would hold interest rates steady despite mounting pressure from member states calling for cuts. The decision reflects the bank's continued concern about inflation in the services sector, which has remained stubbornly above target even as goods inflation has cooled. President Lagarde emphasized during the post-meeting press conference that the bank would remain data-dependent and would not commit to a specific rate path.

Markets responded with modest moves. The euro strengthened against the dollar by 0.4 percent in the hour following the announcement, while European equities gave back early gains to close slightly lower. Bond yields rose across the curve, with the German two-year climbing 8 basis points.

Analysts were divided on the outlook. Goldman Sachs maintained its call for a first rate cut in September, arguing that services inflation would converge to target over the summer. Morgan Stanley pushed its forecast out to December, citing sticky wage growth and the labor market's resilience. Both firms agreed that the ECB is unlikely to move before the Federal Reserve, which continues to signal patience of its own.

Member state governments have been increasingly vocal. French Finance Minister said the current policy stance was "too restrictive for the real economy" and was costing jobs. Italian officials echoed the concern. Germany's finance ministry took the opposite position, warning against premature easing and citing recent upticks in producer prices.

The next policy decision is scheduled for July. Between now and then, the bank will receive two rounds of consumer price data and an updated set of staff projections. Observers say the July meeting will be the first credible window for a cut, though Lagarde's remarks on Tuesday made clear that no decision has been made.

Inside the Governing Council, the split is reportedly widening. Hawkish members have pointed to the risk of a renewed inflation surge if services prices do not continue to cool. Doves argue that holding too long will deepen the recession risk and push inflation below target by the end of the year. Reconciling those views will require more data than the bank currently has.
