# skimr vs Sumy — quality review (2026-04-20)

10-corpus evaluation using two methodologies in parallel:

- **A1 (primary):** qualitative 5-dimension rubric (1–5 per dim, max 25/summary, max 250/summarizer). Dimensions — key-fact coverage, action-item / holding preservation, structure, dedup, non-triviality vs `text[:N]`.
- **A2 (supporting color):** token/bigram overlap vs human-written gold references under `benchmarks/references/` (stopwords removed, lowercased, 3+ char tokens).

Corpus spans 10 document types chosen to stress different structural patterns: front-loaded (CRM, news, meeting notes, wiki), heading-heavy (tech spec, privacy policy, sci paper), split-ends (support ticket), and holdings-at-end (SCOTUS opinion).

Raw outputs: [`outputs-2026-04-20.md`](outputs-2026-04-20.md). Raw ROUGE: [`rouge-2026-04-20.md`](rouge-2026-04-20.md).

## Headline finding

**A1 and A2 disagree on the leaderboard — and A1 is the one to trust.**

| Rank | A1 (rubric /250) | A2 (avg R1-F) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **sumy/TextRank (206)** | skimr/tfidf (0.430) |
| 2 | **sumy/LexRank (204)** | sumy/TextRank (0.409) |
| 3 | **sumy/LSA (197)** | sumy/LexRank (0.376) |
| 4 | **skimr/textrank (193)** | skimr/textrank (0.375) |
| 5 | **skimr/tfidf (186)** | sumy/LSA (0.340) |

skimr/tfidf scores **first on ROUGE but last on rubric**. The inversion is entirely explained by heading-heavy documents (privacy-policy, tech-spec, wikipedia-article, scientific-paper) where skimr's TF-IDF scorer rewards *repeated terms* — which happen to be the words that appear most often in section headings. Result: skimr picks the headings themselves ("History", "Information We Collect", "### Risks") instead of the content under them. The selected heading lines share vocabulary with the reference summary (because the headings name the topics), inflating ROUGE. But the output is nearly content-free — a dressed-up table of contents. That's the A1 collapse in the 12/25 to 15/25 range on those four corpora.

## Per-corpus narrative

**`crm-note-long` and `crm-note-short`** — skimr's strongest corpus class. Front-loaded, action-item-heavy, short-to-medium length. skimr/tfidf captures all four next-step bullets where sumy/LexRank drops them entirely (15/25 — lost all action items to the "main walkthrough" description).

**`meeting-minutes`** — tie between skimr/tfidf (22) and sumy/LexRank (23). LexRank edges out by catching the staging/prod timeline sentence that skimr misses; skimr matches on action items. LSA collapses (15) by dropping action items entirely, exactly the previous-session finding.

**`news-article`** — five-way tie in the 19–20 band. No summarizer does a standout job; all miss something important (Goldman/MS forecast split, markets detail, July date). Candidate for a cue-phrase/temporal-entity boost.

**`privacy-policy` and `tech-spec`** — skimr collapses. Heading-heavy docs where "Information We Collect", "### Risks", and similar short heading-like sentences rank high on TF-IDF because their content tokens repeat throughout the document. skimr returns `heading + tiny fragment + heading + fragment`. sumy/TextRank (22) and sumy/LexRank (20) pick actual rule sentences. **This is the largest structural gap the eval surfaced.**

**`scientific-paper`** — skimr/textrank wins outright (25/25). Grabs the premise sentence from the abstract, the fix sentence from the discussion, and the recommendation from the conclusion — a natural "three-peak" extractive summary. skimr/tfidf (15) loses again to the same heading-picking failure ("Introduction" / "Conclusion" as content-free heading lines).

**`scotus-opinion`** — skimr/tfidf wins (23/25), which was NOT predicted. Position bias should have failed on a holdings-at-end document, but the TF-IDF scorer surprises by picking the "Held:" line and the disposition ("reversed") — these sentences contain the key case terms ("permit", "penalties", "statute") that repeat throughout the opinion, so their TF-IDF scores are high despite being at the end. **Accidental win, not a repeatable one** — would likely break on a different opinion with less terminological concentration.

**`support-ticket`** — sumy/LexRank (24) captures the diagnosis sentence + one workaround + the Resolution line. skimr/tfidf (22) picks the ticket header + original problem + the closing note, missing the middle diagnosis entirely. Split-ends docs favor graph methods.

**`wikipedia-article`** — sumy/LexRank and sumy/TextRank tie at 25/25. Both pick the clean definition sentence, the tradeoff explanation, and the HNSW paragraph — an ideal encyclopedic summary. skimr/tfidf (15) loses again to heading-picking (`History`, `Applications`).

## Why A2 ROUGE misled

**ROUGE rewards lexical overlap. The reference summary mentions "headings" topics by name ("Information We Collect" appears in both the reference and the privacy policy's section header), so when skimr returns headings verbatim, ROUGE registers a match.** The output is nearly useless — no rules, no rights, no retention numbers — but the ROUGE F1 comes in at 0.333, higher than LexRank's 0.248 (which returned actual rules).

This is the classic critique of ROUGE in the summarization literature: lexical fidelity ≠ semantic utility. For skimr's downstream uses (RAG prep, previews, LLM pre-summarization), lexical fidelity to a human-written summary is noise; what matters is *whether the key facts and commitments made it in*. A1 measures that; A2 doesn't.

**Recommendation:** report A1 as primary quality metric. Keep A2 only as supporting color that demonstrates the ROUGE/rubric divergence — a useful cautionary data point but not a leaderboard signal.

## Structural failure patterns surfaced

1. **Heading-picking (heavy).** skimr/tfidf loses ~7 rubric points per heading-heavy document by selecting section titles as sentences. Affects privacy-policy, tech-spec, scientific-paper, wikipedia-article. **Mitigation candidate (Thread C): filter/demote sentences that are markdown headings (`^#+ `), short lines ending in `:`, or ALL-CAPS short sentences before they enter the scoring loop.**

2. **Action-item loss (moderate).** sumy/LSA and sumy/LexRank sometimes drop action items / resolution lines. skimr/tfidf catches them consistently thanks to position bias (action items usually last in a structured doc, but their keyword density is also high so TF-IDF scores them well). **Thread C candidate: cue-phrase boost for sentences starting with "Action items:", "Resolution:", "Held:", "In summary:", "The key finding:".**

3. **Holdings-at-end robustness (lucky).** skimr's accidental win on SCOTUS suggests that TF-IDF rewards terminological-density sentences even at the end of a doc. But this is fragile — the Held line happened to repeat the case's core terms. A structurally similar opinion with abstract reasoning at the end would likely fail. **Thread C candidate: explicit "holding sentence" / "conclusion sentence" detection via cue phrases.**

4. **Split-ends docs underperform.** Support tickets have "problem at top, resolution at bottom" shape; skimr grabs the top and misses the resolution. Position bias isn't enough — a split-aware selector that picks from both halves would help. **Thread C candidate: paragraph-aware or section-aware selection that guarantees coverage from multiple parts of the document.**

5. **Numeric-density matters but isn't rewarded in default mode.** skimr/tfidf often picks heading-ish sentences over sentences containing concrete numbers (`$120K`, `75,000 events/second`, `7 years post-closure`). The keyword-scored mode already has a `+0.3` digit bonus; the default TF-IDF mode does not. **Thread C candidate: port the digit bonus into the default scorer or expose as a config.**

## What changed my mind about skimr's position bias

The prior-session hypothesis was that skimr's position bias would be its main failure mode. This eval shows the **larger failure mode is heading-picking from TF-IDF term repetition, not position bias itself**. Position bias actually protects skimr on front-loaded docs and holdings-at-end docs. The bigger fix is heading filtering + cue-phrase boost, not reworking the position scorer.

## Implications for Thread C (scoring features)

The eval motivates the following priority order for Thread C, ranked by impact on A1 rubric score:

1. **Heading filter** — biggest single win. Would lift skimr/tfidf ~4–7 points on four corpora. Should default-on; config flag to disable.
2. **Cue-phrase boost** — boost sentences with `^(held:|resolution:|in summary:|the key|conclusion:)`. Modest lift on structured docs; large lift on legal/scientific.
3. **Digit bonus in default mode** — carry over from keyword mode. Moderate lift on numeric-heavy docs (tech-spec, privacy-policy retention numbers).
4. **Paragraph-aware selection** — split-ends coverage. Moderate lift on support tickets and longer docs with structured sections.
5. **Section-position weighting** — boost sentences from "Discussion", "Conclusion", "Resolution", "Held", and equivalents. Mitigates the "abstract-only" selection pattern on scientific papers.

Metadata extraction (dates, amounts, named entities) is out of scope for this rubric — it's a separate extractor primitive, not a scoring tweak. Belongs in its own Thread C sub-item and in chunkshop's metadata-extractors brief.

## Methodology notes

- **A1 scoring is the author's.** Transparent, reproducible in structure (the dimensions and score meanings are fixed), but subjective per cell. A multi-rater A1 would be stronger; for a solo eval this is the honest limitation.
- **A2 reference summaries are LLM-generated (Claude Opus 4.7 via this session).** Kept simple, fact-focused, covering what a careful human reader would keep. Stored in `benchmarks/references/` for reproducibility and for future Thread C before/after comparisons.
- **No summarizer was tuned for this corpus.** All run at default parameters with a shared budget (500 chars for skimr/tfidf; 3 sentences for sumy and skimr/textrank).
