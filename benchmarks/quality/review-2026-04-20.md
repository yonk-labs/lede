# lede vs Sumy — quality review (2026-04-20)

10-corpus evaluation using three methodologies:

- **A1 (primary):** qualitative 5-dimension rubric (1–5 per dim, max 25/summary, max 250/summarizer). Dimensions — key-fact coverage, action-item / holding preservation, structure, dedup, non-triviality vs `text[:N]`.
- **A2 (supporting color):** token/bigram overlap vs human-written gold references under `benchmarks/references/` (stopwords removed, lowercased, 3+ char tokens).
- **A4 (independent cross-check):** LLM-as-judge pairwise ranking via a LAN-local Qwen3-Coder (int4) vLLM server. Chosen specifically because Qwen is cross-family to both the reference-writer (Anthropic) and the candidate summarizers, reducing in-family bias.

Corpus spans 10 document types chosen to stress different structural patterns: front-loaded (CRM, news, meeting notes, wiki), heading-heavy (tech spec, privacy policy, sci paper), split-ends (support ticket), and holdings-at-end (SCOTUS opinion).

Raw outputs: [`outputs-2026-04-20.md`](outputs-2026-04-20.md). Raw ROUGE: [`rouge-2026-04-20.md`](rouge-2026-04-20.md).

| 1 | **sumy/TextRank (206)** | lede/tfidf (0.430) |
| 2 | **sumy/LexRank (204)** | sumy/TextRank (0.409) |
| 3 | **sumy/LSA (197)** | sumy/LexRank (0.376) |
| 4 | **lede/textrank (193)** | lede/textrank (0.375) |
| 5 | **lede/tfidf (186)** | sumy/LSA (0.340) |

lede/tfidf scores **first on ROUGE but last on rubric**. The inversion is entirely explained by heading-heavy documents (privacy-policy, tech-spec, wikipedia-article, scientific-paper) where lede's TF-IDF scorer rewards *repeated terms* — which happen to be the words that appear most often in section headings. Result: lede picks the headings themselves ("History", "Information We Collect", "### Risks") instead of the content under them. The selected heading lines share vocabulary with the reference summary (because the headings name the topics), inflating ROUGE. But the output is nearly content-free — a dressed-up table of contents. That's the A1 collapse in the 12/25 to 15/25 range on those four corpora.

## Per-corpus narrative

**`crm-note-long` and `crm-note-short`** — lede's strongest corpus class. Front-loaded, action-item-heavy, short-to-medium length. lede/tfidf captures all four next-step bullets where sumy/LexRank drops them entirely (15/25 — lost all action items to the "main walkthrough" description).

**`meeting-minutes`** — tie between lede/tfidf (22) and sumy/LexRank (23). LexRank edges out by catching the staging/prod timeline sentence that lede misses; lede matches on action items. LSA collapses (15) by dropping action items entirely, exactly the previous-session finding.

**`news-article`** — five-way tie in the 19–20 band. No summarizer does a standout job; all miss something important (Goldman/MS forecast split, markets detail, July date). Candidate for a cue-phrase/temporal-entity boost.

**`privacy-policy` and `tech-spec`** — lede collapses. Heading-heavy docs where "Information We Collect", "### Risks", and similar short heading-like sentences rank high on TF-IDF because their content tokens repeat throughout the document. lede returns `heading + tiny fragment + heading + fragment`. sumy/TextRank (22) and sumy/LexRank (20) pick actual rule sentences. **This is the largest structural gap the eval surfaced.**

**`scientific-paper`** — lede/textrank wins outright (25/25). Grabs the premise sentence from the abstract, the fix sentence from the discussion, and the recommendation from the conclusion — a natural "three-peak" extractive summary. lede/tfidf (15) loses again to the same heading-picking failure ("Introduction" / "Conclusion" as content-free heading lines).

**`scotus-opinion`** — lede/tfidf wins (23/25), which was NOT predicted. Position bias should have failed on a holdings-at-end document, but the TF-IDF scorer surprises by picking the "Held:" line and the disposition ("reversed") — these sentences contain the key case terms ("permit", "penalties", "statute") that repeat throughout the opinion, so their TF-IDF scores are high despite being at the end. **Accidental win, not a repeatable one** — would likely break on a different opinion with less terminological concentration.

**`support-ticket`** — sumy/LexRank (24) captures the diagnosis sentence + one workaround + the Resolution line. lede/tfidf (22) picks the ticket header + original problem + the closing note, missing the middle diagnosis entirely. Split-ends docs favor graph methods.

**`wikipedia-article`** — sumy/LexRank and sumy/TextRank tie at 25/25. Both pick the clean definition sentence, the tradeoff explanation, and the HNSW paragraph — an ideal encyclopedic summary. lede/tfidf (15) loses again to heading-picking (`History`, `Applications`).

## Why A2 ROUGE misled

**ROUGE rewards lexical overlap. The reference summary mentions "headings" topics by name ("Information We Collect" appears in both the reference and the privacy policy's section header), so when lede returns headings verbatim, ROUGE registers a match.** The output is nearly useless — no rules, no rights, no retention numbers — but the ROUGE F1 comes in at 0.333, higher than LexRank's 0.248 (which returned actual rules).

This is the classic critique of ROUGE in the summarization literature: lexical fidelity ≠ semantic utility. For lede's downstream uses (RAG prep, previews, LLM pre-summarization), lexical fidelity to a human-written summary is noise; what matters is *whether the key facts and commitments made it in*. A1 measures that; A2 doesn't.

**Recommendation:** report A1 as primary quality metric. Keep A2 only as supporting color that demonstrates the ROUGE/rubric divergence — a useful cautionary data point but not a leaderboard signal.

## Structural failure patterns surfaced

1. **Heading-picking (heavy).** lede/tfidf loses ~7 rubric points per heading-heavy document by selecting section titles as sentences. Affects privacy-policy, tech-spec, scientific-paper, wikipedia-article. **Mitigation candidate (Thread C): filter/demote sentences that are markdown headings (`^#+ `), short lines ending in `:`, or ALL-CAPS short sentences before they enter the scoring loop.**

2. **Action-item loss (moderate).** sumy/LSA and sumy/LexRank sometimes drop action items / resolution lines. lede/tfidf catches them consistently thanks to position bias (action items usually last in a structured doc, but their keyword density is also high so TF-IDF scores them well). **Thread C candidate: cue-phrase boost for sentences starting with "Action items:", "Resolution:", "Held:", "In summary:", "The key finding:".**

3. **Holdings-at-end robustness (lucky).** lede's accidental win on SCOTUS suggests that TF-IDF rewards terminological-density sentences even at the end of a doc. But this is fragile — the Held line happened to repeat the case's core terms. A structurally similar opinion with abstract reasoning at the end would likely fail. **Thread C candidate: explicit "holding sentence" / "conclusion sentence" detection via cue phrases.**

4. **Split-ends docs underperform.** Support tickets have "problem at top, resolution at bottom" shape; lede grabs the top and misses the resolution. Position bias isn't enough — a split-aware selector that picks from both halves would help. **Thread C candidate: paragraph-aware or section-aware selection that guarantees coverage from multiple parts of the document.**

5. **Numeric-density matters but isn't rewarded in default mode.** lede/tfidf often picks heading-ish sentences over sentences containing concrete numbers (`$120K`, `75,000 events/second`, `7 years post-closure`). The keyword-scored mode already has a `+0.3` digit bonus; the default TF-IDF mode does not. **Thread C candidate: port the digit bonus into the default scorer or expose as a config.**

## What changed my mind about lede's position bias

The prior-session hypothesis was that lede's position bias would be its main failure mode. This eval shows the **larger failure mode is heading-picking from TF-IDF term repetition, not position bias itself**. Position bias actually protects lede on front-loaded docs and holdings-at-end docs. The bigger fix is heading filtering + cue-phrase boost, not reworking the position scorer.

## Implications for Thread C (scoring features)

The eval motivates the following priority order for Thread C, ranked by impact on A1 rubric score:

1. **Heading filter** — biggest single win. Would lift lede/tfidf ~4–7 points on four corpora. Should default-on; config flag to disable.
2. **Cue-phrase boost** — boost sentences with `^(held:|resolution:|in summary:|the key|conclusion:)`. Modest lift on structured docs; large lift on legal/scientific.
3. **Digit bonus in default mode** — carry over from keyword mode. Moderate lift on numeric-heavy docs (tech-spec, privacy-policy retention numbers).
4. **Paragraph-aware selection** — split-ends coverage. Moderate lift on support tickets and longer docs with structured sections.
5. **Section-position weighting** — boost sentences from "Discussion", "Conclusion", "Resolution", "Held", and equivalents. Mitigates the "abstract-only" selection pattern on scientific papers.

Metadata extraction (dates, amounts, named entities) is out of scope for this rubric — it's a separate extractor primitive, not a scoring tweak. Belongs in its own Thread C sub-item and in chunkshop's metadata-extractors brief.

## A4 — LLM-as-judge cross-check (Qwen3-Coder-Next, int4)

Added after the initial review to address: does an independent judge agree with the A1 rubric, or was the rubric biased by the author's own scoring? The judge is **Intel/Qwen3-Coder-Next-int4-AutoRound** running on a LAN-local vLLM server. Qwen is from Alibaba — outside both the OpenAI lineage (sumy's training-data family, loosely) and the Anthropic lineage (which wrote the reference summaries in A2). Cleanest cross-family judge available.

**Method:** each corpus, 5 summaries anonymized as A–E in randomized order, judge ranks 1–5 on (1) fact coverage, (2) action-item / holding preservation, (3) coherence — with an explicit instruction that length is not a reward signal. Temperature 0, seed 42. Total: 10 judgments, 11,917 tokens (free — runs on-prem).

Script: [`benchmarks/quality_eval_llm.py`](../quality_eval_llm.py). Detailed output: [`llm-judge-2026-04-20.md`](llm-judge-2026-04-20.md).

### What A4 validates

**A1 and A4 agree on the extremes.** sumy/TextRank wins both (#1 in each). lede/tfidf loses both (#4 or #5). **An independent, cross-family judge independently confirms the rubric finding** — the A1 result isn't a scoring-bias artifact.

**A2 ROUGE is the outlier.** Both the human rubric and the cross-family LLM judge rank lede/tfidf near the bottom; ROUGE ranks it #1. This is now empirically — not just theoretically — a ROUGE failure mode, not a quality signal.

### What A4 disagrees with A1 on

- **lede/textrank moves up** (A1 #4 → A4 #2). Qwen likes its compressed middle-of-doc selections more than my rubric did. Possible interpretation: A1's "non-triviality vs text[:N]" dimension weighted structural layout; Qwen weighs informational density. Both valid; the delta is small.
- **sumy/LSA moves down** (A1 #3 → A4 #5). Qwen dislikes LSA's tendency to pick tangential mid-doc content that humans find unusual. A1 gave LSA partial credit for non-triviality; Qwen didn't.
- **lede/tfidf rank is stable at the bottom** (A1 #5 → A4 #4). The failure mode is confirmed by both methods.

### Per-corpus pattern — where lede/tfidf wins vs loses (A4)

**lede/tfidf wins (A4 1st place):**
- `crm-note-long` (1st) — front-loaded operational doc
- `crm-note-short` (1st) — very front-loaded

**lede/tfidf competitive (A4 2nd place):**
- `meeting-minutes` (2nd, behind lede/textrank) — structured doc with clear lede
- `news-article` (2nd, behind sumy/LSA) — inverted pyramid

**lede/tfidf collapses (A4 5th place, 5 of 10 corpora):**
- `scientific-paper` (5th) — findings in Discussion, not abstract
- `scotus-opinion` (5th) — holdings at end; A1's accidental-win-on-terminology did NOT replicate under A4
- `support-ticket` (5th) — split-ends, resolution at bottom
- `tech-spec` (5th) — heading-heavy
- `wikipedia-article` (5th) — Qwen noticed that lede returned headings instead of HNSW/history content

**Strong signal:** the failure is **structural**, not accidental. Every corpus where lede/tfidf ranks 5th is a document type where the lede is either (a) empty (support ticket), (b) misleading (sci paper abstract is preamble, not findings), (c) polluted by headings (tech spec, wiki, privacy policy), or (d) literally at the end (scotus). That's exactly the failure-mode analysis the A1 rubric surfaced — validated now by an independent judge.

### Implications for Thread C confidence

The prior A1 finding — heading-picking is the highest-value fix — is **now confirmed by three independent lenses**:

1. **Author rubric (A1)** — human pattern recognition with dimension-level breakdowns.
2. **Token overlap (A2)** — unreliable, but its *divergence pattern* itself is diagnostic (lede rewards the wrong thing; a fix should reduce the A2-vs-A1 gap).
3. **Cross-family LLM judge (A4)** — independent validation with a different bias profile.

All three say **sumy/TextRank > lede/tfidf at current defaults**. A1 + A4 together also say the **gap is concentrated in heading-heavy and split-ends structures**. That's a triangulation strong enough to justify shipping the Thread C fixes in v0.1.1 without further evaluation.

## Methodology notes

- **A1 scoring is the author's.** Transparent, reproducible in structure (the dimensions and score meanings are fixed), but subjective per cell. A4 serves as the independent cross-check.
- **A2 reference summaries are LLM-generated (Claude Opus 4.7 via this session).** Kept simple, fact-focused, covering what a careful human reader would keep. Stored in `benchmarks/references/` for reproducibility and for future Thread C before/after comparisons.
- **A4 judge is Qwen3-Coder (Intel int4 quant) on a LAN-local vLLM server.** Cross-family to the reference writer (Anthropic) and to sumy's training data (broadly OpenAI-era corpora). One judgment per corpus; low-variance due to temperature 0 and seed 42, but a multi-seed average would be tighter. Left as follow-up if the Thread C fixes produce ambiguous before/after signal.
- **No summarizer was tuned for this corpus.** All run at default parameters with a shared budget (500 chars for lede/tfidf; 3 sentences for sumy and lede/textrank).
